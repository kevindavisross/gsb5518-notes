Problem 1

Xavier and Yolanda are playing roulette. They both place bets on red on the same 3 spins of the roulette wheel before Xavier has to leave. (Remember, the probability that any bet on red on a single spin wins is 18/38.) After Xavier leaves, Yolanda places bets on red on 2 more spins of the wheel. Let \(X\) be the number of bets that Xavier wins and let \(Y\) be the number that Yolanda wins.

  1. Compute and interpret \(\text{P}(X + Y = 4)\).

  2. Make a table representing the marginal distribution of \(X+Y\).

  3. Use the table from the previous part to compute \(\text{E}(X+Y)\), and verify that it is equal to \(\text{E}(X)+\text{E}(Y)\). (You computed \(\text{E}(X)\) and \(\text{E}(Y)\) in a previous homework.)

  4. Without doing any computation, determine if \(\text{Var}(X+Y)\) will be greater than, less than, or equal to \(\text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\). Explain your reasoning.

  5. Use the marginal distribution of \(X+Y\) (from earlier in this problem) to compute \(\text{Var}(X+Y)\).

  6. In a previous HW you identified the marginal distribution of \(X\) by name. Use facts about that distribution to compute \(\text{Var}(X)\).

  7. In a previous HW you identified the marginal distribution of \(Y\) by name. Use facts about that distribution to compute \(\text{Var}(Y)\).

  8. Verify that \(\text{Var}(X+Y)\) is equal to \(\text{Var}(X)+ \text{Var}(Y)+2\text{Cov}(X,Y)\). (You computed the covariance in a previous homework.)

\(x\), \(y\) 0 1 2 3 4 5
0 0.0404 0.0727 0.0327 0 0 0
1 0 0.1090 0.1963 0.0883 0 0
2 0 0 0.0981 0.1766 0.0795 0
3 0 0 0 0.0294 0.0530 0.0238

Solution

Solution

  1. \(X\) has a Binomial distribution with parameters \(n=3\) and \(p = 18/38\), so \(\text{E}(X) = np = 3(18/38) = 1.42\).

  2. \(Y\) has a Binomial distribution with parameters \(n=5\) and \(p = 18/38\), so \(\text{E}(X) = np = 5(18/38) = 2.37\).

  3. In the first 3 bets Yolanda wins the same number of games as Xavier, and Yolanda only plays two more games. So \(Y\) can’t be more than \(X+2\), and \(Y\) can’t be less than \(X\).

  4. To have \(X = 2\) and \(Y=3\) we need to have exactly two wins in the first 3 bets (where both play) exactly 1 win in the last 2 bets (Yolanda only). The number of wins in the first 3 bests is Binomial(3, 18/38), the number of wins in the last two bets is Binomial(2, 18/38) and the first 3 bets are independent of the last two bets. So the probability is dbinom(2, 3, 18 / 38) * dbinom(1, 2, 18 / 38) = r round(dbinom(2, 3, 18 / 38) * dbinom(1, 2, 18 / 38), 3) or \[ p_{X, Y}(2, 3) = \text{P}(X = 2, Y = 3) = \left(\binom{3}{1}(18/38)^2(20/38)^1\right)\left(\binom{2}{1}(18/38)^1(20/38)^1\right) = 0.177 \]

  5. No. \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. For example \(\text{P}(X = 0) = 0.15\) but \(\text{P}(X=0|Y = 0) = 1\).

  6. Sum over the \((X, Y)\) pairs for which \(X + Y = 4\): (0, 4), (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), (4, 0). So 0 + 0.0883 + 0.0981 + 0 = 0.1864. Suppose they do this every time they visit the casino. Over many visits to the casino, together they win a total of 4 bets in 18.6% of visits

  7. The possible values are \(0, 1, \ldots, 8\). Fill in the probabilities like in the previous part. (Alternative \(X + Y\) has the same distribution as \(2X + Z\) where \(X\) and \(Z\) are independent and \(Z\) (number of wins in last two bets) has a Binomial(2, 18/38) distribution).

  8. From the table \(\text{E}(X+Y) = 0(0.404) + 1(0.0727 + \cdots + 8(0.0238) = 3.79\), and \(\text{E}(X)+\text{E}(Y) = 3(18/38) + 5(18/38) = 3.79\). (These are exactly equal; any differences are just rounding errors)

Problem 2

A vendor at a weekend farmers market sells three products: jars of honey, loaves of sourdough bread, and bags of ice. Let \(X\) be the number of jars of honey sold, \(Y\) the number of loaves sold, and \(Z\) the number of bags of ice sold. Based on several seasons of sales data, the number of units sold on a given Saturday follows a joint distribution with

  • Mean 40 and standard deviation 8 for honey jars sold,
  • Mean 25 and standard deviation 5 for bread loaves sold,
  • Mean 15 and standard deviation 6 for ice bags sold,
  • Correlation of 0.5 between honey jars sold and bread loaves sold,
  • Correlation of −0.4 between honey jars sold and ice bags sold. (Cooler days tend to bring out more shoppers browsing specialty foods like honey, but fewer people buying ice.)

Honey jars sell for $12 each, bread loaves sell for $7 each, and ice bags sell for $4 each.

  1. Let \(R_1 = 12X + 7Y\) be the vendor’s revenue from honey and bread combined. Compute \(E(R_1)\) and \(SD(R_1)\).

  2. Let \(R_2 = 12X + 4Z\) be the vendor’s revenue from honey and ice combined. Compute \(E(R_2)\) and \(SD(R_2)\).

  3. Without doing any additional calculation, explain whether you’d expect each of \(SD(R_1)\) and \(SD(R_2)\) to be larger or smaller if the variables were uncorrelated.

Solution

Part 1

\[ E(R_1) = 12E(X) + 7E(Y) = 12(40) + 7(25) = 480 + 175 = 655 \]

\[\begin{align*} Var(R_1) & = 12^2 SD(X)^2 + 7^2 SD(Y)^2 + 2(12)(7)\,\text{Corr}(X,Y)\,SD(X)\,SD(Y)\\ & = 144(64) + 49(25) + 2(12)(7)(0.5)(8)(5)\\ SD(R_1) & = \sqrt{12121} \approx 110.10 \end{align*}\]

Part 2

\[ E(R_2) = 12E(X) + 4E(Z) = 12(40) + 4(15) = 480 + 60 = 540 \]

\[\begin{align*} Var(R_2) & = 12^2 SD(X)^2 + 4^2 SD(Z)^2 + 2(12)(4)\,\text{Corr}(X,Z)\,SD(X)\,SD(Z)\\ & = 144(64) + 16(36) + 2(12)(4)(-0.4)(8)(6)=7949\\ SD(R_2) & = \sqrt{7949} = 89 \end{align*}\]

Part 3

Since honey jars sold and ice bags sold are negatively correlated, \(SD(R_2)\) would be smaller than it would be if they were uncorrelated. Both coefficients in \(R_2 = 12X + 4Z\) are positive, so a negative correlation between \(X\) and \(Z\) means that when one variable is unusually high, the other tends to be unusually low — the two terms partially offset each other’s variability rather than reinforcing it. This makes the covariance term \(2(12)(4)\,\text{Corr}(X,Z)\,SD(X)\,SD(Z)\) negative, which lowers the total variance (and therefore the standard deviation) compared to the uncorrelated case.

Problem 3

Devi and Paxton are meeting. Arrival times are measured in minutes after noon, with negative times representing arrivals before noon. Devi’s arrival time follows a Normal distribution with mean 20 and SD 15 minutes, and Paxton’s arrival time follows a Normal distribution with mean 25 and SD 10 minutes.

All of the probability calculations will involve a Normal distribution, but make sure you clearly identify the correct mean and SD. You should also compute standardized values and use a table to make a ballpark estimate of probabilities before jumping straight to software/calculator.

You will compute all of the following probabilities in each of the 3 scenarios below.

  1. The probability that Devi arrives before noon.
  2. The probability that Devi arrives before Paxton.
  3. The probability that Devi arrives before Paxton given that Paxton arrives at 12:10.
  4. The probability that the first person to arrive has to wait more than 15 minutes for the second person to arrive.

  1. Scenario 1: Assume the arrival times are independent

  2. Scenario 2: Assume the pairs of arrival times follow a Bivariate Normal distribution with correlation 0.8

  3. Scenario 3: Assume the pairs of arrival times follow a Bivariate Normal distribution with correlation -0.7

Solution

Part a) is the same in all three scenarios. If \(V\) is Devi’s arrival time, \(V\sim N(20, 15)\). We want \(\text{P}(V < 0)\). The standardized value for 0 is \((0 - 20)/15=-1.333\); that is, 0 is 1.333 SDs below the mean. From the empirical rule, 87% of values are within 1.5 SDs of the mean, so our estimated probability is more than 6.5%. From software, \(\text{P}(V < 0)=0.09\).

Part b) If \(X\) is Paxton’s arrival time, we want \(\text{P}(X > V) = \text{P}(X - V >0)\). Since \(X\) and \(V\) have a Bivariate Normal distribution, then \(X - V\) has a Normal distribution. The mean is \(20-15 = 5\). The only difference in the three scenarios is the variance, which affects the probability.

Scenario 1: \(X\) and \(V\) are independent \[ \text{Var}(X - V) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(V) - 2\text{Cov}(X, V) = 10^2 + 15^2 - 0 = 325 \] So \(\text{SD}(X - V) = \sqrt{85} = 18.0\). We want \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)\). The standardized value for 0 is \((0 - 5)/18=-0.277\); that is, 0 is 0.277 SDs below the mean. The probability will be more than 50%. From software, \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)=0.61\).

Scenario 2: \[ \text{Var}(X - V) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(V) - 2\text{Cov}(X, V) = 10^2 + 15^2 - 2(0.8)(10)(15) = 85 \] So \(\text{SD}(X - V) = \sqrt{85} = 9.2\). We want \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)\). The standardized value for 0 is \((0 - 5)/9.2=-0.54\); that is, 0 is 0.54 SDs below the mean. The probability will be more than in Scenario 1. From software, \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)=0.71\).

Scenario 3: \[ \text{Var}(X - V) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(V) - 2\text{Cov}(X, V) = 10^2 + 15^2 - 2(-0.7)(10)(15) = 535 \] So \(\text{SD}(X - V) = \sqrt{535} = 23.1\). We want \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)\). The standardized value for 0 is \((0 - 5)/23.1=-0.216\); that is, 0 is 0.216 SDs below the mean. The probability will be less than in Scenario 1. From software, \(\text{P}(X - V > 0)=0.59\).

Part c) \(\text{P}(V < X | X = 10) = \text{P}(V < 10 | X = 10)\). The probability depends on the conditional distribution of \(V\) given \(X=10\) which will be Normal but the mean and SD depend on the correlation.

Scenario 1: Since \(X\) and \(V\) are independent, \(\text{P}(V < 10 | X = 10)=\text{P}(V < 10)\). This is similar to part a. \(V\sim N(20, 15)\). We want \(\text{P}(V < 10)\). The standardized value for 10 is \((10 - 20)/15=-0.67\); that is, 0 is 0.67 SDs below the mean. From software, \(\text{P}(V < 10)=0.31\).

Scenario 2: Paxton’s time is 1.5 SDs below the mean, \(\frac{10 - 25}{10} = -1.5\). Devi’s conditional mean arrival time is 1.2 SDs below the mean, \(0.8(-1.5) = -1.2\), so Devi’s conditional mean arrival time is \(\text{E}(V | X = 10) = 20 -1.2(15) =2\). Devi’s conditional SD is \(\text{SD}(V|X = 10) = 15\sqrt{1-0.8^2} = 9\). So \((V | (X = 10))\sim N(2, 9)\). \(\text{P}(V < 10 | X = 10) = \text{P}(Z < \frac{10-2}{9}) = \text{P}(Z < 0.889)\); ballpark estimate a little less than 84%. Software (below) gives 0.81.

Scenario 3: Paxton’s time is 1.5 SDs below the mean, \(\frac{10 - 25}{10} = -1.5\). Devi’s conditional mean arrival time is 1.05 SDs above the mean, \((-0.7)(-1.5) = 1.05\), so Devi’s conditional mean arrival time is \(\text{E}(V | X = 10) = 20 +1.05(15) =35.75\). Devi’s conditional SD is \(\text{SD}(V|X = 10) = 15\sqrt{1-0.7^2} = 10.7\). So \((V | (X = 10))\sim N(35.75, 10.7)\). \(\text{P}(V < 10 | X = 10) = \text{P}(Z < \frac{10-35.75}{10.7}) = \text{P}(Z < -2.4)\); ballpark estimate is about 0.005. Software (below) gives 0.008. The probability is small because if Paxton arrives early then Devi is expected to arrive late, so Devi is unlikely to be the first to arrive.

Part d) is similar to Part b. We want \(\text{P}(|X - V| >15) = \text{P}(X - V > 15) + \text{P}(X - V < -15)\). We have already found the distributon of \(X-V\) is part b: it’s Normal with mean 5 and SD that depends on the scenario. So we standardized each of 15 and -15 to get the two tail probabilities and then add them together to get the overall probability (though most of the probability is in the “Devi arrives 15 minutes before Paxton” tail).

Scenario 1: The standardized value for 15 is \((15 - 5) / 18 = 0.556\). The standardized value for -15 is \((-15 - 5) / 18 = -1.11\). The probability that a value is more than 1 SD below the mean is about 0.16; the probability that a value is more than 0.5 SDs above the mean is about 0.31. So the probability that we want is less than 0.47. It’s \(\text{P}(|X - V| > 15) = 0.42\).

Scenario 2: The probability should be less than in Scenario 1 because with positive correlation they tend to arrive close together. The standardized value for 15 is \((15 - 5) / 9.2 = 1.09\). The standardized value for -15 is \((-15 - 5) / 9.2 = -2.2\). The probability that a value is more than 1 SD above the mean is about 0.15; the probability that a value is more than 2 SDs below the mean is about 0.02. So the probability that we want is around 0.17. From sofware, it’s \(\text{P}(|X - V| > 15) = 0.15\).

Scenario 3: With a negative correlation, when one arrives early the other tends to arrive late, so the probability will be higher than in Scenario 1. The standardized value for 15 is \((15 - 5) / 23.1 = 0.433\). The standardized value for -15 is \((-15 - 5) / 23.1 = -0.866\). The probability that a value is more than 0.4 SD above the mean is about 0.32; the probability that a value is around 1 SD below the mean is about 0.17. So the probability that we want is around 0.49. From sofware, it’s \(\text{P}(|X - V| > 15) = 0.52\).

Problem 4

Consider the general problem of estimating a population proportion \(p\) for some binary (success/failure) categorical variable of interest. Select a sample of size \(n\) and let \(X\) be the number of successes in the sample.

  1. We are considering a general problem here, but describe a specific context where you might want to do this: what is the population and its rough size, what is the variable of interest and what is “success”, what is the sample, how do you measure \(X\) for the sample? (For example, the handouts covered the general problem of estimating \(\mu\) for a Poisson population, but we thought of it in terms of the car dealership problem.)
  2. Suggest an estimator of \(p\); it should be an expression involving \(X\) and \(n\). Describe in words what your estimator is. (Hint: what is the most obvious estimator you can think of?)
  3. Suppose you observe \(x = 6\) successes in a sample of size \(n=10\). Use your estimator to compute a point estimate of \(p\) based on this sample.
  4. Consider the estimator (called the “plus four” estimator) \[ \frac{X + 2}{n + 4} = \left(\frac{4}{n+4}\right)\left(0.5\right) + \left(\frac{n}{n+4}\right)\left(\frac{X}{n}\right) \] Explain in words what this estimator does. (The two expressions in the equation above are just different ways of expressing the estimator.)
  5. Suppose you observe \(x = 6\) successes in a sample of size \(n=10\). Use the plus four estimator to compute a point estimate of \(p\) based on this sample.
  6. If you observe \(x = 6\) successes in a sample of size \(n=10\), which of your two estimates is a better estimate of \(p\)? Explain.

Solution

  1. Say the population is all American adults (roughly 300 million), the variable is whether or not the adult supports government-provided health care (yes = “success”), the parameter \(p\) is the population proportion of US adults who support government-provided health care. Survey a random sample of \(n=1000\) adults, let \(X\) be the number in the sample (out of 1000) who support health care.
  2. One estimator of \(p\) is the sample proportion \(\hat{p}=X/n\).
  3. For this sample the sample proportion is \(\hat{p}=6/10 = 0.6\).
  4. The “plus four” estimator basically appends 2 successes and 2 failures to the sample data and then computes the sample proportion based on the appended sample. (It is also a weighted average between the observe sample proportion \(X/n\) and 0.5.)
  5. Based on the 6/10 sample, the plus four estimator estimates \(p\) to be \((6+2)/(10+4) = 8/14 = 0.571 = (10/14)(0.6) + (4/14)(0.5) = 0.571\).
  6. We don’t know which of 0.6 and 0.571 is a better estimate because we don’t know what \(p\) is; that’s the reason why we’re trying to estimate it.
